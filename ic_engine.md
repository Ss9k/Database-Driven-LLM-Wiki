# Internal Combustion Engine

An Internal Combustion Engine (ICE) is a heat engine that converts the chemical energy stored in fuel into mechanical work by burning the fuel inside a combustion chamber.

The engine consists of a cylinder, piston, connecting rod, crankshaft, valves, spark plug (in petrol engines), and fuel injector. During combustion, expanding gases push the piston downward. The connecting rod transfers this motion to the crankshaft, producing rotational motion.

A four-stroke petrol engine operates through four stages: intake, compression, power, and exhaust. During the intake stroke, the piston moves downward while the intake valve opens, allowing an air-fuel mixture into the cylinder. During compression, both valves remain closed while the piston compresses the mixture. Near the end of compression, the spark plug ignites the mixture. Combustion rapidly increases pressure and forces the piston downward during the power stroke. Finally, the exhaust valve opens and burnt gases leave the cylinder during the exhaust stroke.

Diesel engines differ from petrol engines because they compress only air before injecting diesel fuel. High temperature due to compression causes self-ignition without requiring a spark plug.

The combustion chamber is the region where fuel burns and high-pressure gases are generated. Proper combustion improves efficiency while reducing emissions.

Internal combustion engines are widely used in automobiles, motorcycles, generators, ships, agricultural machinery, and industrial equipment.

Advantages include high power density, mature manufacturing technology, and quick refueling. Disadvantages include greenhouse gas emissions, noise, vibration, and dependence on fossil fuels.
